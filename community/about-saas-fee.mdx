Saas-Fee has evolved from a remote farming community into one of Switzerland's most celebrated Alpine resorts — yet it has retained its Walliser character. The traditional chalets, narrow lanes, and close-knit community give it an authenticity that larger, more commercialised resorts often lack. When you walk through the village, you're moving through a living settlement with centuries of history beneath its feet — not a purpose-built resort constructed around a ski lift.

## History

The Saas Valley has been inhabited since the Middle Ages, when Alemannic settlers made their way into the high alpine terrain that now defines the region. For centuries, the valley's economy rested almost entirely on farming and cattle grazing — the steep pastures and summer alps sustained families through each generation, shaping the resourceful, self-sufficient character that still defines the community today.

The outside world began to take notice in the 19th century. Mountaineers seeking to explore and conquer the peaks of the Mischabel range — which includes Dom, the highest entirely Swiss mountain — discovered the Saas Valley as a natural base. The first mountain guides and guesthouses emerged to serve them, planting the seeds of what would become a world-class tourism industry.

By the mid-20th century, the pace of change accelerated. Funicular railways and early ski infrastructure opened the mountains to a wider public, and Saas-Fee's reputation as a ski destination began to grow rapidly. In 1951, the village made a decision that would define its identity for generations: Saas-Fee went car-free, becoming one of the first Alpine resorts in the world to ban private motor vehicles from its streets. That single commitment to people over cars has shaped everything that followed.

## Walliser Culture

Saas-Fee sits in the canton of Valais — known in German as Wallis — and the culture of this region is distinct and proud. The Valais is German-speaking in its eastern reaches (where Saas-Fee lies), with a strong tradition of folk music, communal festivals, and regional cuisine that sets it apart from the rest of Switzerland.

Traditional dress, known as **Tracht**, is still worn at community events and festivals. Far from being a tourist performance, it reflects a genuine continuity of local identity. When you see Tracht at a village celebration, you're witnessing a community choosing to honour its own heritage.

The local dialect spoken in the village is **Wallisertitsch** — a form of Alemannic German that is noticeably different from standard Swiss German and can be difficult even for other German speakers to follow. You'll hear it in everyday conversations between locals, in the bakery, and at the market. It's one of the more tangible reminders that Saas-Fee is, first and foremost, a real community.

Community life here has traditionally revolved around the village church, the weekly market, and the rhythm of seasonal festivals — a pattern that remains largely intact despite the pressures of modern tourism.

## Architecture

Saas-Fee's built environment is one of its most distinctive features. The traditional wooden chalets that line the village lanes are not merely decorative — they are the original homes, barns, and storehouses of an alpine farming community, many of which have been carefully maintained or sensitively restored over the centuries.

The most iconic architectural element unique to the Saas Valley and Upper Valais is the **Heidenhaus** — a dark-stained timber granary raised off the ground on stone pillars topped with flat mushroom-shaped discs. These raised platforms, known as *Stadel*, were an ingenious solution to a practical problem: by placing smooth stone discs between the pillars and the granary floor, builders prevented rodents from climbing up into stored grain. Several beautifully preserved examples can be found right in the village centre, a reminder that the buildings you see are functional architectural history, not reconstruction.

New construction in Saas-Fee is subject to planning guidelines that respect this traditional aesthetic. The result is a village that feels cohesive and rooted — a rare quality in a destination of international significance.

## Language & Communication

The official language of Saas-Fee is German, and you'll find that most signs, menus, and official communications lead with German. However, English is widely spoken throughout the resort — in hotels, restaurants, ski schools, and shops — and you should have no difficulty communicating as an English-speaking visitor.

French and Italian, Switzerland's other national languages, are also understood by many residents and hospitality workers, reflecting both the country's multilingual culture and the village's cosmopolitan workforce. Road signs and most menus are typically presented in German and English, with some establishments adding French for completeness.

## Local Governance

Saas-Fee is a **Gemeinde** — a municipality — within the canton of Valais. Day-to-day governance is handled by the **Gemeinderat** (village council), a locally elected body that oversees planning, infrastructure, community services, and the village's relationship with the broader tourism industry.

What makes Saas-Fee's governance model noteworthy is the degree of local control retained over resort development. Decisions on tourism policy, sustainability commitments, planning approvals, and community investment are made at the village level, not handed down from a distant corporate structure. The community has a genuine voice in how Saas-Fee evolves — and that sense of ownership is part of what keeps the village feeling authentic rather than managed.

## Religion & Customs

Saas-Fee is a predominantly Roman Catholic community, and the village church — a handsome structure at the heart of the settlement — acts as both a spiritual and social focal point. Religious observance is woven naturally into the fabric of village life rather than existing separately from it.

The major religious festivals of the Catholic calendar are celebrated with real community participation. Easter, Christmas, and **Corpus Christi** in particular are marked with processions through the village streets, attended by residents in traditional dress and accompanied by the local brass band. These are not tourist events — they are genuine expressions of community faith and identity that you are welcome to observe respectfully.

Sunday is still observed as a day of relative rest in many respects: some local businesses keep shorter hours, and the pace of village life is noticeably quieter. It's a rhythm worth embracing rather than working around.
