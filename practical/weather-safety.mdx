Saas-Fee's Alpine environment is beautiful but demands respect. Weather can change rapidly at altitude, and conditions that seem mild in the village can be severe at 3000m. A sunny morning in the resort centre can turn into a whiteout on the glacier within an hour. This guide helps you understand the risks, prepare appropriately, and know exactly what to do if something goes wrong during your visit.

## Mountain Weather

Alpine weather follows patterns that are distinct from lowland forecasts — understanding them helps you plan safer, more enjoyable days on the mountain.

- **Morning clarity:** mountain days are typically clearest in the morning. Cloud and mist tend to build through the day, so starting early rewards you with the best visibility and snow conditions.
- **Afternoon thunderstorms:** in summer especially, afternoon thunderstorms are common and can develop rapidly. If you're on a high ridge or glacier after midday, keep an eye on the sky and be prepared to descend quickly.
- **Winter snowstorms:** heavy snowfall can reduce visibility to near zero on the upper mountain. These conditions can develop with relatively little warning — check the forecast the night before every day you plan to ski or hike above the village.
- **Temperature drop with altitude:** as a rule of thumb, temperature falls by approximately **6.5°C for every 1000m of altitude gain**. If it's 5°C in Saas-Fee village (1800m), expect around -5°C at the Mittelallalin top station (3456m). Dress accordingly.
- **Wind chill:** wind speed increases with altitude. A calm day in the village can mean biting, exposed conditions on the glacier. Factor wind chill into your clothing decisions.

For accurate, up-to-the-hour forecasts, use **MeteoSwiss** — the official Swiss meteorological service at [meteoswiss.ch](https://www.meteoswiss.ch). Their mountain-specific forecasts are far more reliable than general weather apps for Alpine conditions.

## Sun & UV Protection

The combination of altitude and snow reflection makes UV radiation in Saas-Fee's ski area dramatically more intense than at sea level — and it catches many visitors off guard.

- **Altitude amplifies UV:** for every 1000m of elevation, UV radiation increases by approximately 10–12%. At 3000m you're exposed to roughly 25% more UV radiation than at sea level.
- **Snow reflection:** fresh snow reflects up to 80% of UV radiation back upwards. This means you can burn from both above and below — underneath your chin, nostrils, and the inside of your wrists are classic spots people miss.
- **SPF 50+ is the minimum** for any time spent on the glacier or on open pistes. Don't be tempted to use a lower factor on overcast days — cloud cover blocks very little UV radiation.
- **UV-protective sunglasses or goggles** are essential. Without proper eye protection, you risk snow blindness (photokeratitis) — a painful, temporary condition caused by UV damage to the cornea.
- **Glacier terrain:** the exposed nature of glacier skiing means there's no tree cover or terrain to provide shade. Cover up with a buff or neck gaiter and consider a helmet with a visor.
- **Reapply every two hours**, and after sweating or wiping your face. Most people apply sunscreen once in the morning and consider it done — this is not enough at altitude.

## Avalanche Awareness

<Warning>
  Avalanche risk exists in the mountains around Saas-Fee. Always check the daily avalanche bulletin at **[slf.ch](https://www.slf.ch)** (the WSL Institute for Snow and Avalanche Research) before off-piste skiing or ski touring. Carry an avalanche transceiver (ARVA/beacon), probe, and shovel when venturing off marked pistes, and ensure you know how to use them before leaving the resort boundary. **Never ski off-piste alone.**
</Warning>

### The European Avalanche Danger Scale

Avalanche risk is communicated using a standardised 5-level scale across Europe. Check this rating each morning before heading out:

| Level | Danger | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Low** | Green | Generally safe conditions; isolated natural avalanches possible only on very steep terrain |
| **2 — Limited** | Yellow | Triggering possible on steep slopes, particularly at specific aspects or elevations; caution required |
| **3 — Considerable** | Orange | **Most avalanche fatalities occur at this level.** Human triggering likely on steep slopes; natural avalanches possible |
| **4 — High** | Red | Very likely triggering on most steep slopes; large natural avalanches possible; stay on marked runs |
| **5 — Extreme** | Black | Widespread natural avalanche activity; extraordinary avalanche deposit sizes expected; do not leave the resort |

**Marked pistes are controlled and regularly inspected** by the resort's avalanche safety team. Ski patrol uses explosive charges and other methods to trigger controlled avalanches before the lifts open, and regularly assesses slope conditions throughout the day. Staying on marked, open pistes is the single most effective way to protect yourself from avalanche risk.

## Altitude Effects

Saas-Fee village sits at **1800m** and the ski area tops out at **3456m** at Mittelallalin, reached via the Metro Alpin funicular. Mild altitude sickness — formally known as Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) — can affect even fit, healthy visitors, particularly on rapid ascent to the glacier.

**Common symptoms include:**
- Headache (most common)
- Fatigue and general lethargy
- Breathlessness on minor exertion
- Nausea or loss of appetite
- Disturbed sleep

**How to reduce the risk:**

1. **Ascend gradually** — don't take the Metro Alpin to 3456m on your first morning if you've driven up from sea level the previous day. Give your body 24 hours to adjust at village altitude first.
2. **Stay well hydrated** — dry Alpine air and exertion cause you to lose fluid faster than you might expect. Drink water consistently throughout the day.
3. **Take it easy on day one** — avoid heavy aerobic exercise on your first day at altitude. A gentle acclimatisation walk at village level is a better start than a full day of hard skiing at 3000m.
4. **Avoid alcohol** — alcohol dehydrates you and impairs your body's acclimatisation response. It's best avoided entirely for the first 24 hours after arrival at altitude.
5. **Descend if symptoms worsen** — mild AMS typically resolves with rest at lower altitude. If symptoms are severe or worsen despite descending, seek medical attention.

The village medical centre can provide advice and treatment for altitude-related symptoms. Don't push through significant symptoms — listen to your body.

## Cold Weather & Hypothermia

At altitude in Saas-Fee, the risk of hypothermia is real even on days that feel manageable in the village. Cold, wet, and wind combine to drain body heat faster than most people expect.

**Dress in three layers:**

<CardGroup cols={3}>
  <Card title="Base Layer" icon="shirt">
    A moisture-wicking thermal layer — merino wool or synthetic — that pulls sweat away from your skin. Avoid cotton, which holds moisture and chills you rapidly when wet.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Mid Layer" icon="cloud-snow">
    An insulating layer — fleece, down jacket, or synthetic fill — that traps warm air close to your body. This layer can be removed and packed away if you're generating heat on a long ski run.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Outer Layer" icon="wind">
    A waterproof and windproof shell jacket and salopettes. Look for sealed seams and adequate ventilation zips (pit zips) for adjusting your temperature on the go.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

**Keep extremities covered.** Heat loss from your head, neck, hands, and feet is disproportionately high. A warm hat or helmet liner, neck gaiter, and well-insulated gloves or mittens are not optional — they're essential.

**Recognise the early signs of hypothermia:**
- Persistent, uncontrollable shivering
- Confusion or difficulty thinking clearly
- Slurred speech
- Loss of coordination or stumbling
- Unusual fatigue or drowsiness

If you or someone in your group shows these signs, **descend and seek warmth immediately**. Remove wet clothing, replace with dry layers, and give warm (not hot) drinks if the person is conscious. Seek medical help without delay.

## On-Slope Emergency Procedure

If an accident occurs on the mountain, staying calm and following a clear procedure makes an enormous difference to the outcome. Save the REGA number (**1414**) in your phone before you head out.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Stop in a Safe Location">
    Move yourself and any injured person out of the path of other skiers or boarders — to the side of the piste or behind a natural windbreak. Do not leave an injured person alone unless you have no other choice.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Mark the Scene">
    If someone is injured and unable to move, mark the scene by crossing two skis upright in the snow approximately 10 metres uphill of the casualty. This is the universal mountain signal for an injured person and alerts approaching skiers to slow down.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Call for Help">
    Call **1414** (REGA — Swiss Air Rescue) directly, or alert the nearest lift station or ski patrol post. On the mountain, lift operators can contact ski patrol faster than you might expect. The REGA app can automatically send your GPS location when you place a call.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Keep the Person Warm and Still">
    While waiting for rescue, keep the injured person as warm as possible — use spare clothing, a rescue blanket (carried in most ski first aid kits), or body heat. Do not give food or drink to someone who may have a head or abdominal injury. Keep the person still and reassure them that help is on the way.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Provide Your Location Clearly">
    When speaking to rescuers, give your location as precisely as possible. Use the nearest **piste marker number** (the blue or orange numbered posts you'll see along every run), the name of the run you're on, your GPS coordinates from your phone's maps app, or a description relative to a known landmark or lift station.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
  Download the **MeteoSwiss app** and the **SLF avalanche bulletin app** before your trip. Both provide real-time, location-specific updates that are far more reliable than general weather platforms for Alpine conditions. The REGA app is also worth installing — it can transmit your GPS coordinates automatically in an emergency, which can be critical in poor visibility or unfamiliar terrain.
</Tip>
